A team marks a security repair complete. A manager approves an AI pilot. A university pauses spending. Each action changes something, but the original problem may still need attention.

Before calling the work finished, check what changed and what remains unresolved. This week's sources offer four places to make that distinction. They concern exposed systems, AI safeguards, recurring costs, and early student support. The useful habit is to define the evidence needed to close a problem.

> [!NOTE] Signal: Check What Changed Before Calling It Fixed
>
> Match the evidence of completion to the problem you meant to solve.

## What To Do Next

Use a short completion check on one open decision:

1. **Name the problem.** Describe the risk or barrier in terms another person can check.
2. **Separate the actions.** List what was installed, approved, paused, or offered.
3. **Check the result.** Ask which evidence shows the original problem has changed.
4. **Keep the open question visible.** Assign a next check and a date if the evidence is still incomplete.

The check should fit the stakes. A small reversible choice needs less proof than a decision that could expose people to serious harm.

## What This Looks Like

A security team can record a patch as installed while keeping a separate investigation open. The two records answer different questions.

A budget owner can label a hiring pause as temporary relief. The next review asks which costs return when the pause ends.

An adviser can record that a student received a referral. A later check asks whether the student reached the service and resolved the barrier.

These are proposed local checks, not reported results from the sources.

## 1. Check Exposure Before Closing a Security Ticket

CISA's November 13 notice reports two additions to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities Catalog. The additions rest on evidence of active exploitation. Its guidance points to federal rules that prioritize high-risk systems exposed to the public internet. Those rules include checking for compromise before repair.

The notice shows why installing a fix and investigating an exposed system need separate records. It does not establish that a reader's system has been compromised. The binding directive applies to U.S. federal civilian executive branch agencies. CISA encourages other organizations to use the catalog in their own risk reviews.

Ask the responsible security team to check the product, its exposure, and the applicable response guidance. Keep the repair status separate from the investigation status. A product match can justify further work; an absent product can change the decision.

> [!WARNING] Close the right question
>
> A repair record shows what changed. It does not, by itself, settle what happened before the change.

**Next question:** Which evidence is still needed before the responsible team can close the incident?

## 2. Test AI Safeguards Before Production Approval

Kristin Burnham's November 9 MIT Sloan article reports four areas where companies need AI guardrails. They cover project selection, workforce support, suppliers, and the move into everyday use. The article asks leaders to consider data readiness, system connections, running costs, and the ability to explain and monitor results.

This guidance shows what an approval review can overlook when it focuses only on a successful demonstration. It does not prove that these safeguards guarantee a safe or profitable system. It is research-informed management guidance, not a controlled test of every recommended practice.

Before approving a pilot for regular use, test one foreseeable failure with the staff who must respond. Check who can pause the service, inspect a disputed result, and contact the supplier. Include the time and cost of that response in the approval decision.

> [!TIP] Test the difficult case
>
> A smooth demonstration leaves an important question open: can the team recognize and handle the case that goes wrong?

**Next question:** What must the team demonstrate beyond a successful output before you approve regular use?

## 3. Check Which Savings Survive the Next Budget

A November 9 BU Today interview reports revenue $42 million below Boston University's budget expectations. The chief financial officer separates one-time measures from changes intended to address recurring pressures. She also explains why endowment assets and reserves are not all available for ongoing expenses.

That figure describes a shortfall against expected revenue, not the university's total loss or deficit. The interview shows how the institution explains its position. It does not prove that proposed changes have already produced lasting savings. Nor does one university's account establish the financial condition of higher education as a whole.

For one service, divide proposed savings by duration. Identify costs that return next year and costs needed to make a change. Check whether work moves to another unit. Keep those effects visible before treating a temporary measure as a recurring saving.

> [!IMPORTANT] Keep time in the calculation
>
> Money saved once can buy time. A recurring promise needs resources that remain available.

**Next question:** Which saving disappears when normal activity resumes, and what replaces it?

## 4. Check Whether Early Help Reaches Students

Kauffman's November 13 feature reports how Youth Lead KC developed its support for students in Kansas City. A summer intake process identified money gaps, practical barriers, and difficulties with college systems before the next stage began. The group paired follow-up and data systems with relationships students already trusted.

The account shows a concrete change in when staff look for barriers. It does not prove that the process improved graduation or retention. Kauffman is the funder describing a supported organization. The local example offers a comparison for other programs, not a result they can assume.

Choose one early point where students can ask for help. Check whether they know the route, receive a response, and reach the service. A completed form is only the first step. If a barrier remains unresolved, review the handoff before adding another intake question.

**Next question:** How would you know that a student reached help, beyond knowing that a referral was sent?

## Leaders

People can feel pressure to close a task before they can close the problem. Make it acceptable to report both a completed action and an unresolved question. Ask what evidence would settle the next decision, and keep the request small enough to answer.

Farnam Street's November 15 issue offers a related thinking prompt: revise your view when evidence changes, whoever supplies it. That is a reflection on judgment, not empirical support for the four signals.

> [!LEADERS] Leave room for an unfinished answer
>
> Let the status report say what is done, what is unknown, and who can resolve the difference.

## The Tension

Waiting for perfect evidence can delay a useful repair. Closing too early can hide a serious problem. Separate immediate action from final assurance. Take the justified first step, then set a proportionate check for the question that remains.

## Questions Worth Asking

- What did we complete, and what problem did we actually change?
- Which claim depends on evidence we have not yet seen?
- Who can tell us that the proposed fix failed in practice?
- What would justify closing, continuing, or changing the response?

## What To Watch Next

Look for evidence that distinguishes an action taken from a problem resolved.
