This week's signals point to one operating gap: useful information, formal commitments, and outside support often stop before they become action.

Managers can collect feedback without delivering it clearly. New leaders can hold authority without local knowledge. Frontline workers can see a broken handoff before executives do. Health records can connect without giving a clinician usable information. A company can publish a responsible-business policy while purchasing routines point another way.

The public signal is not simply "communicate more."

The signal is this: make every important handoff ownable. Name the information, the receiver, the decision, the owner, and the proof that the transfer worked.

> [!NOTE] Signal: Make Every Handoff Ownable
>
> Information becomes useful when someone owns its transfer into a decision, routine, and visible follow-up.

---

## The Handoff Test

Use this test when information or a commitment has to move into action.

1. **Name the source.** What does the evidence or promise actually say?
2. **Name the receiver.** Who needs it in order to act?
3. **Name the decision.** What choice or routine should change?
4. **Name the owner.** Who is accountable for the transfer and follow-up?
5. **Name the proof.** What would show the handoff worked?

> [!TIP]
> Shared information is not the same as owned use.

## What To Do Next

Map one important handoff before adding another meeting, dashboard, policy, or training session.

Put the unit and boundary beside every headline measure.

Translate one public commitment into an incentive, routine, owner, and review date.

Ask a partner what capability should remain after outside support leaves.

## 1. Name the Owner Before Information Changes Hands

Management Science reports experimental evidence about performance feedback. The work shows that managers may withhold useful feedback or make it too vague to guide improvement. Wharton Executive Education adds a role-transition lesson: formal authority does not replace local relationships and knowledge.

Harvard Business Review reports the frontline version. Workers often see customer friction, broken tools, and slow handoffs early. The OECD shows the technical version in healthcare. Connected records create value only when people, policy, process, and technology make the information usable.

This evidence does not prove one communication process works for every team. It supports a clear management decision: assign the receiver, decision right, owner, and follow-up before the handoff begins.

> [!QUOTE]
> The person closest to the constraint often sees the second consequence first.

**Next question:** Who owns the transfer from this information to the next decision?

---

## 2. Name the Unit Before You Use the Number

The U.S. Census Bureau reports frequent business-condition evidence through its Business Trends and Outlook Survey. Brookings shows why the unit behind a public measure matters. Household and individual measures of benefit participation can answer different questions and produce different estimates.

Census also reports household conditions through HTOPS, including work, food, insurance, mental health, connectedness, prices, and expenses. The agency labels the survey experimental and states design limits. Harvard Business Review adds the workplace version: ordinary task counts can leave out cognitive, emotional, and coordination work.

This evidence does not prove every dashboard or public measure is wrong. It supports a better measurement decision: name the unit, boundary, missing population, and invisible work before using a number as a judgment.

> [!WARNING]
> A precise number can still be incomplete.

**Next question:** Which person, task, definition, or handoff is outside this measure?

---

## 3. Turn Commitments Into Operating Routines

The OECD reports a large gap between responsible-business policies and disclosed operating practice. Its outlook covers 10,000 large listed companies in 52 countries. Many companies report policies. Far fewer report that social supply-chain policies shape purchasing or that safety improved.

GAO shows the same implementation problem in public training and education. A training goal needs a cycle of needs, delivery, evaluation, and mission use. A teaching or inclusion mandate needs materials, support, environment, and follow-through.

This evidence does not prove that every undisclosed practice is absent. It does not establish one reason for every gap. It supports a practical business decision: connect each promise to incentives, resources, purchasing choices, review, and observable results.

> [!TIP]
> A value becomes credible when the operating system makes it easier to practice.

**Next question:** Which routine would prove that this commitment reaches real work?

---

## 4. Build Local Capability Before Support Leaves

The World Bank reports on rural agri-entrepreneur hubs in Bangladesh that bring services closer to farmers. MIT reports that its cybersecurity clinic has completed more than 40 assessments while giving students real partner work. Brookings describes small-business support as an ecosystem problem rather than one isolated program.

The World Bank also reports on 20 community-led efforts in rural Morocco across agriculture, tourism, crafts, and digital work. These program accounts show design and activity. This evidence does not prove long-term causal impact or that the same model belongs in every place.

The evidence supports a service and community decision: build knowledge, tools, ownership, and a review routine that the partner can use after outside support leaves.

> [!LEADERS]
> Measure what the partner can keep doing, not only what the project delivered.

**Next question:** What capability should remain useful six months after this support ends?

---

## Leaders

Leadership this week is about owning the space between knowing and doing.

That does not mean the leader personally handles every transfer. It means someone is named, the decision right is clear, and the organization can see whether the information changed the work.

Make the handoff visible enough to question. Make the owner clear enough to follow up. Make the evidence limit honest enough to protect judgment.

## The Tension

Clear ownership can improve follow-through. It can also become a way to blame one person for a poorly designed system.

The goal is not to assign a name and walk away. The goal is to connect responsibility with authority, information, time, tools, and a fair review.

Ownership without support becomes blame. Support without ownership becomes drift.
