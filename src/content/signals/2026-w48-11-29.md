A shop owner has a line at the counter on Saturday and a supplier on the phone on Monday. The weekend tally is real. How much more stock should she buy? That depends on something the tally leaves out. Which customers are likely to come back?

For this November 29 advance edition, we followed the evidence through Saturday, November 28. Choose a decision you face this week. Write down what you know today, then name the one observation that would make you choose differently.

> [!NOTE] Signal: Choose What Would Change Your Decision
>
> A good time to agree on what would change a decision is before the new numbers arrive.

## What To Do Next

Start with a choice that is still open. Ask the person responsible when the answer is due.

1. **Name the options.** Write the choice as two actions. For a shop they might be a small reorder and a large one.
2. **Set the threshold.** Name one observation that would make the second action worth its cost.
3. **Choose a deadline.** Set a date to check. If the evidence will arrive too late, decide how much you can safely commit now.

Try the exercise with someone who would make a different choice today. Ask what evidence would bring your estimates closer.

## What This Looks Like

Ask the person making a forecast to finish this sentence: “I would change my estimate if we saw …” Keep their answer beside the forecast. It gives the next conversation a place to start.

## 1. Check which students count toward the fine

At UNC-Chapel Hill, 104 students made a difference of nearly $5 million. Carolina Public Press reports that the university exceeded its cap on out-of-state freshmen for a second year. Multiplying the 104 places by $47,472 in base tuition gives the reported penalty of $4,937,088.

Western Carolina exceeded its own cap by 83 students. It had stayed within the cap the year before and avoided that fine. The comparison shows why a head count alone can mislead a budget committee. It does not tell applicants which rule will apply to next year’s class.

Before approving an enrollment forecast, check the rule for that cohort and rerun the calculation. Keep a proposed rule on a separate line.

**Next question:** Which change in the rule or the cohort would move the budget?

## 2. Check the route before changing the shipment

At a November 23 Security Council meeting, UN officials reported disruption to Black Sea shipping and grain exports. They also described damage to Ukraine’s energy grid as winter approached. For someone booking freight, the report raises a question about a real route.

The meeting record shows what officials reported. It cannot tell a buyer whether a particular carrier will cancel a sailing or what a buyer will pay for grain. Check the booked departure with the carrier. Price a backup while there is still time to use it.

**Next question:** Which documented change would justify paying for the alternate route?

## 3. Name the test behind the milestone

NASA reports that all seven DragonCam cameras had been installed, tested and integrated into its Titan-bound rotorcraft by mid-November. The team could point to images from the cameras. Three more science instruments still faced delivery, integration and testing.

The images show progress on the camera suite. They cannot show that the craft is ready for launch. Think of a software team celebrating a working payment screen while refunds still need testing. Check what the completed test lets you approve and which decision must wait.

**Next question:** What is the smallest part of the project you can sign off today?

## 4. Measure how many customers come back

The Small Business Administration identifies November 28 as Small Business Saturday. Its page invites shoppers to support independent businesses. A full shop on Saturday gives the owner a pleasant problem on Monday. How much stock should they reorder?

The observance shows why shoppers had a reason to visit. It does not tell the owner which customers will come back at the usual price. Choose a small reorder that can be adjusted. Count how many dated return offers went out and how many came back before placing a larger order.

**Next question:** What evidence of a second visit would change your next order?

## Leaders

If a colleague brings you a number, ask them to walk you from the number to the proposed action. Listen for the assumption they need along the way. Give them time to check it before asking for a firmer recommendation.

> [!LEADERS] Leave room to revise
>
> Agree on the evidence that warrants a change while the team can still collect it.

## The Tension

Waiting for better information has a cost. A backup booking can expire. A shop can run out of stock. Price the cost of waiting alongside the cost of being wrong.

## Questions Worth Asking

- What does this observation let us approve today?
- Who can supply the missing detail before the decision is due?
- How much can we commit while keeping the other option open?
