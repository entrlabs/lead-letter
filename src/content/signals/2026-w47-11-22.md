Imagine a research team that publishes a smoke map every Monday. The old satellite images are safe in an archive. Next Monday, someone still has to find a fresh image to make the map.

This week, choose one change your team is about to make. Trace the work to someone who depends on it. Ask them to try the first task they will face after the change, while you still have time to adjust the plan.

> [!NOTE] Signal: Plan for the People Who Depend on You
>
> A transition plan gets more precise when the person on the receiving end can try it.

## What To Do Next

1. Name one person outside your team who relies on its work.
2. Ask what they need next, how often they need it and what happens if it arrives late.
3. Try their first task together. Give the unresolved question to someone who can make a decision before the change takes effect.

## What This Looks Like

An archive can answer what happened last summer. A fresh observation can help someone decide where to send a crew today. List those two uses separately before promising that the old data will meet everyone’s needs.

## 1. Plan for the end of a data feed

NASA says Terra will stop collecting science data on December 1 after more than 27 years. The archive will remain available. A team studying smoke can still compare old observations. A team waiting for tomorrow’s observation faces a different problem.

The announcement shows why keeping a long record and replacing a live feed require different plans. It does not show that another instrument can provide the same measurement at the same time or scale.

Choose one report that relies on fresh observations. Ask its owner to run it using the proposed replacement. Record how a gap or a change in resolution affects the decision that follows.

**Next question:** Which decision needs a new observation, and when will the old feed stop supplying it?

## 2. Give returning students a route through credit review

Higher Ed Dive reports that 37.8 million working-age adults in the United States had left college before earning a credential. The estimate comes from National Student Clearinghouse Research Center data. A former student who wants to finish still needs an answer about prior credits.

The count shows the size of a population. It does not tell an advisor which course a particular student needs or which change will bring students back.

Choose one former student’s path with their permission, or use a fictional transcript. Have two offices trace it from the first inquiry to a written credit decision. Record every point where each office expects the other to answer.

**Next question:** Who can give a returning student an answer they can use to choose a course?

## 3. Test shared services from a second site

SOFX reports that 17 of 22 FAA en route centers experienced communications problems during an incident involving a router in Arkansas. A person responsible for one site may have little visibility into the experience of a team elsewhere.

The account shows the reported reach of the disruption. It does not establish the final cause or show that a particular test would have prevented it.

For your next shared-service change, ask a colleague at a second site to try one essential task. Decide in advance who can pause the rollout and how they will reach the person making the change.

**Next question:** Which site can see a failure that the installation team would miss?

## 4. Give participants one decision they can change

A Kauffman Foundation essay asks who defines a problem, controls resources and decides how success is measured. Picture a neighborhood partner invited to design evening workshops after the building hours and staffing budget have been set. What is still open?

The essay shows how to examine the terms of an invitation. It does not show that a particular governance arrangement improves trust. The question is whether the partner can influence a decision that matters to the people attending.

Before sending the invitation, name one choice the group can make. Put the budget limit and the decision date beside it. Ask the partner whether that choice is worth the time you are asking them to give.

**Next question:** What would change in the program if participants chose a different option?

## Leaders

The person most familiar with a system can miss the simplest dependency. A customer may rely on a date in an email. An advisor may need the name of the office that can approve an exception. Ask someone outside the project to describe the transition in their own words. Listen for the point where they stop knowing what to do.

> [!LEADERS] Make time for a trial
>
> Invite feedback early enough for someone to change the decision that caused the problem.

## The Tension

An early trial costs time. A late discovery can leave a school, customer or partner carrying that cost instead. Choose a test small enough to run before the decision and specific enough to change it.

## Questions Worth Asking

- Which part of the service do we expect people to replace on their own?
- What will they need on the first day that the old arrangement is gone?
- Who can change the plan if the trial goes badly?
