Your team has a full week planned. Then a new project arrives, a colleague leaves, or a deadline moves forward. Everyone agrees the new work matters. The calendar still has the same number of hours, and the old tasks still have owners waiting for results.

Before you add the request, decide what will stop or shrink. This week's brief offers a way to make that choice while protecting needed skills. It also examines two mistakes that can distort the decision: hearing only from easy-to-reach people and using a broad number to answer a narrow question.

> [!NOTE] Signal: Decide What Stops Before Adding Work
>
> Give a new priority room by choosing what loses time and protecting the expertise the remaining work needs.

## What To Do Next

Use these four steps in your next workload discussion.

1. **Name the new task.** State what must be delivered, by when, and who needs it.
2. **Choose the reduction.** Agree which existing task will pause, shrink, or move. Give that change an owner.
3. **Protect a needed skill.** Identify any expert work the cut could remove, including teaching, review, or problem solving.
4. **Set a review date.** Check whether time was freed and whose workload changed.

The test is whether the agreed reduction happens. A shorter task list on a slide does not free anyone's afternoon.

## What This Looks Like

These are possible uses of the method, not measured results from the sources below.

**A team lead** receives an urgent customer request. Before assigning it, she asks which planned report can wait and gets that delay approved.

**A university leader** reviews a support budget. He separates the cost of student appointments from the work of designing courses and evaluating learning.

**A founder** considers a change based on customer feedback. She checks whether the respondents include people who stopped using the service.

## 1. Decide What Stops Before Adding Work

In a September 16 HBR article, executive coach Melody Wilding describes a leader who absorbed his team's overflow. His nights and weekends filled up, leaving less room for the strategic work he was hired to do.

Her guidance shows a practical way to discuss the constraint: ask the team which work can pause or shrink. Then make the decision rules clear and give people authority to act. This is coaching advice built around examples. It does not prove that the method reduces burnout or raises output in every team.

Use it to choose one actual reduction before you add an assignment. Check afterward whether the work disappeared or quietly moved to someone else.

> [!TIP]
> Put the paused task beside the new task in the same written decision. Tell the people who were expecting it.

**Next question:** Who can approve the reduction, and when will the team know it is real?

## 2. Protect Expertise When You Reduce Services

Carl Follmer's September 22 AACSB article examines what universities risk losing when they remove communication-center leadership. Tutoring may continue while the people who shape teaching across courses lose their role.

The article shows why those are separate functions. Specialists help design assignments, work with faculty, and adapt teaching as workplace needs change. It discusses institutional examples and makes a professional argument. It does not establish the effect of any particular closure on student learning.

Before you choose a cut, list both the visible service and the expert work behind it. Then name who would carry each function, with what time and authority. This question helps identify a possible loss; it does not mean every existing role must remain unchanged.

> [!WARNING]
> An appointment count cannot tell you who is improving the curriculum or checking how students develop over time.

**Next question:** After the change, who will have time to improve the service rather than only keep it open?

## 3. Check Whose Views Your Feedback Represents

A team can ask for input and still miss people affected by its choice. Pew Research Center's September 16 report offers a precise example from U.S. political polling.

Pew reports that party-affiliation weighting improved accuracy in its 2020 and 2024 postelection polls by about 1–2 percentage points per candidate. Weighting changes how much different responses count. It depends on a defensible comparison with the population the poll aims to describe.

The report shows why participation matters. It does not prove that political adjustments belong in a workplace or customer survey. The useful decision is to check who answered, who is missing, and what comparison supports the result.

For a local workload review, start with a simpler step: invite input from people whose work is easy to overlook. Treat that as a proposed practice, not a proven extension of the polling result.

**Next question:** Whose experience could change this decision, and have we heard from them?

## 4. Read The Components Before Changing Plans

A national headline can prompt a business question without answering it. BEA's September 24 release reports that the U.S. current-account deficit widened to $246.0 billion in the second quarter of 2026. That was $33.4 billion more than the revised first-quarter figure.

The current account records trade in goods and services alongside income and transfer flows. BEA reports a larger goods deficit, partly offset by smaller income deficits. Both broad receipts and payments rose. The balance alone leaves out those different movements.

This evidence shows the quarter's national transactions. It does not show a particular firm's demand, costs, or staffing needs. Before you change a plan, identify the component relevant to your business and compare it with your own operating data.

Use the release to ask a better question about customers or suppliers. Watch later revisions before treating one estimate as settled.

**Next question:** Which part of this national measure connects to a decision we actually control?

## Leaders

An explicit choice creates room for an honest disagreement. People can challenge a proposed reduction, explain an overlooked function, or identify a cost that falls elsewhere. They cannot do that as easily when every task remains officially urgent.

> [!LEADERS]
> Make the trade-off clear enough for someone affected by it to question it before the work changes.

Farnam Street's September 27 *More Focus* offers a brief reminder that attention deserves deliberate choice. Use that as a reflection prompt. It is not evidence that focus alone solves a staffing shortage.

## The Tension

Keeping every role and task is rarely possible. Cutting the least visible work can also be costly when that work builds skills or prevents errors. A useful review asks what can change now, what must continue, and what would justify reversing the choice.

## Questions Worth Asking

- Which task can stop with the smallest cost to the people we serve?
- What knowledge would leave with the proposed cut?
- Who is missing from the feedback we are using?
- What result would make us revise this choice?
