A plan can be clear, detailed, and wrong about the condition that matters most.

Demand may come from temporary support. A strong strategy may meet an unready team. Wider access may reach an institution with weak follow-through. A faster tool may scale the same human bottleneck.

The answer is not endless planning. It is a simple reality check before the cost of changing direction rises.

> [!NOTE] Signal: Test the Plan Against Reality
>
> Name the assumption that matters most, then collect one direct observation that could change the plan.

---

## What To Do Next

Use this five-part reality check before a launch, expansion, or major commitment.

1. **Name the map.** What report, forecast, strategy, or policy guides the decision?
2. **Find the assumption.** What condition must be true for the plan to work?
3. **Check the people.** Who sees the real conditions sooner than the dashboard does?
4. **Test readiness.** What capability, authority, or support must exist before launch?
5. **Set the revision point.** What evidence will make you change direction?

> [!TIP]
> A plan becomes safer when the team knows how to update it.

---

## What This Looks Like

A founder sees a sales increase after temporary support. Before hiring, the founder checks whether customers return without the lift.

A manager has a strong strategy. Before announcing it, the manager tests whether the team has the authority and skills to execute it.

A college widens eligibility. Leaders then check advising, capacity, persistence, and governance after students enter.

A product team uses AI to generate more ideas. The team also checks whether the tool narrowed the range of ideas people considered.

---

## 1. Test Team Readiness Before Launch

Harvard Business Review reports that leaders can design a strong strategy without creating the conditions needed to execute it. The Kauffman Foundation also shows a planning period used to build partnerships, governance, measures, and readiness before implementation funding.

This evidence shows that execution capacity belongs inside strategy. It does not prove one checklist or planning period works for every team.

The supported decision is to test one required capability, one decision right, and one early warning sign before launch.

> [!LEADERS]
> Do not ask only whether the plan is smart. Ask whether the team can carry it.

**Next question:** What must this team be able to decide or do on the first difficult day?

---

## 2. Check Repeat Demand Before Adding Capacity

The Associated Press business listing reports that U.S. retail sales fell after a summer tax-refund boost faded. The captured page did not expose the full article, so the claim must stay narrow.

The evidence shows that temporary support can change the timing of demand. It does not prove why all U.S. retail sales moved or predict demand for one company.

The supported decision is to check repeat behavior and the source of the lift before hiring, ordering, or expanding.

> [!WARNING]
> A short lift can become a long commitment if you scale before customers return.

**Next question:** How much of this demand remains when the temporary support ends?

---

## 3. Build Coordination Capacity Before Funding Action

The Kauffman Foundation reports that six coalitions received a planning year before one moved into a three-year, $13 million Teach KC implementation grant. The planning work included partnerships, governance, evaluation, and readiness.

This shows that complex work may need coordination capacity before implementation. It does not prove the Teach KC effort will succeed or that every coalition needs the same path.

The supported decision is to check whether partners can decide, measure, learn, and resolve conflict together before adding activity.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Funding buys action. Shared decisions and measures help action become learning.

**Next question:** Can the partners change course together when the first result disappoints them?

---

## 4. Connect Wider Access With Credible Stewardship

Inside Higher Ed reports that Michigan narrowed eligibility differences in a free community-college tuition initiative. It also reports that an accreditor placed Lane Community College on warning over governance issues.

These reports show that access rules and institutional stewardship are separate conditions. The evidence does not prove Michigan’s policy will improve completion or that Lane’s warning explains every campus outcome.

The supported decision is to connect eligibility with advising, capacity, persistence, learning, and clear accountability.

> [!QUOTE]
> Opening the door matters. So does what the institution can support after entry.

**Next question:** What will students need after they become eligible, and who is accountable for providing it?

---

## Leaders

Good leaders use plans to coordinate action. They do not confuse the plan with the world it describes.

Choose the assumption that matters most. Ask the people closest to the work what the map leaves out. Test the capability needed for launch. Then name the evidence that will make you revise.

Commitment matters. So does the ability to change before confidence becomes an expensive mistake.

## The Tension

Plans make coordinated action possible. Constant revision can exhaust a team and hide weak commitment.

But refusing to update a plan can turn discipline into denial. The practical balance is to decide in advance which evidence matters. Do not reopen every choice. Reopen the assumption that can defeat the outcome.

---

## Questions Worth Asking

1. What assumption must be true for this plan to work?
2. Who sees the real conditions before the dashboard does?
3. Is this demand durable, repeated, and independent of temporary support?
4. Does the team have the authority and capability to execute the strategy?
5. What evidence will make us revise rather than defend the plan?
