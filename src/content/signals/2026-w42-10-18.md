A small business loses access to its booking system. Staff can still help customers, but nobody knows which appointments are urgent. A school faces a different interruption: a student leaves, then cannot find a workable route back. In both cases, a plan for normal conditions leaves a crucial question unanswered.

Choose what must continue when the usual route breaks. This week's evidence offers four places to test that question: business disruption, interrupted schooling, automated decisions, and development partnerships. These are different problems. Each asks readers to check whether people can recover useful action after a setback.

> [!NOTE] Signal: Plan What Continues When Conditions Break
>
> Choose essential work and test a route back before people need it under pressure.

## What To Do Next

Use a short interruption exercise with your team this week.

1. **Choose one essential result.** Name what a customer, learner, or partner still needs during a disruption.
2. **Remove one normal condition.** Imagine a tool is unavailable, an application is rejected, or a person must pause participation.
3. **Walk the alternative route.** Check who can help, what information remains available, and where the person gets stuck.
4. **Test the return.** Set a small rehearsal and record what must change before the next attempt.

## What This Looks Like

For a small business, the exercise might be a practice run using an approved backup appointment list. For a school, it might trace a returning learner's first week. These are suggested exercises, not reported results from the sources. Start with one service so the test stays manageable.

## 1. Plan essential work through a disruption

An October 12 Harvard Business Review article reports research involving 1,000 senior executives across 20 industries in 13 countries. Its authors question whether strong defenses and a written continuity plan are enough. The public summary urges firms to prepare for operations while parts of their technology remain unavailable.

That evidence shows why managers should examine how work continues during a cyber disruption. It does not prove that a particular recovery plan works in every firm. The survey methods and full article were not available in the public excerpt checked for this brief.

Choose one essential service and rehearse a limited outage with the people who deliver it. Use approved backup procedures and involve the responsible security staff. Record which decisions stalled and which information staff could safely reach. Watch whether the next exercise reduces those delays.

> [!TIP] Practice the difficult hour
>
> A document describes a plan. A rehearsal reveals the step that people cannot carry out.

**Next question:** Which essential customer need could your team still meet if its usual system became unavailable?

## 2. Plan a return after learning stops

UNESCO's report summary, updated October 14, describes global gains in girls' enrollment alongside persistent barriers to completion. It reports that fewer than 5% of pregnant and parenting girls in sub-Saharan Africa return to school. That regional figure should not be applied to every country or to all learners.

The report shows that formal access can coexist with interrupted education. It does not prove which support would work best in a particular school. Local costs, transport, childcare, safety, and stigma can shape a learner's return in different ways.

Choose one group whose studies have stopped and ask what makes returning difficult. Review the route with those learners before adding another enrollment target. Track return and continued participation, with suitable privacy protections. Watch whether a change reaches the people who previously could not come back.

> [!IMPORTANT] Count the return
>
> Entry is one milestone. A useful education pathway also helps people resume after an interruption.

**Next question:** What would a returning learner have to solve before attending the first week?

## 3. Keep a way to challenge automated decisions

Kristin Burnham's October 13 MIT Sloan article reports researchers' findings and arguments about creating value from AI. It describes a financial-services firm that needed audit records, monitoring, and ways to challenge an AI lending agent's decisions. The account also warns that firms need people with enough expertise to judge the output.

This research summary shows a concrete governance problem: a fast decision can still need correction. It does not prove that the same controls guarantee reliable results everywhere. The underlying research briefing predates this week; the October article supplies a new public explanation, not a new experiment.

Before expanding an automated service, test how a person can question a result. Check whether a qualified reviewer can see the relevant evidence and correct the outcome. Protect time for experts to practice their own work. Watch both correction times and the kinds of errors that keep returning.

> [!WARNING] Keep correction usable
>
> A review button offers little help if nobody can explain or change the result.

**Next question:** Can an affected person reach someone who has the evidence and authority to correct a decision?

## 4. Test the route from partner to delivery

The World Bank Group's October 12 announcement reports the launch of a Philanthropy Hub in Bangkok. It describes one entry point for philanthropic capital, knowledge, and partners. More than 200 leaders attended the related forum, according to the institution.

The release shows that a new partnership channel was launched. It does not prove that the channel has improved development outcomes. A meeting count measures attendance, while a delivered project requires further decisions and work.

A foundation or community organization can use the announcement to ask practical questions about its own partnerships. Choose one proposal and trace its route from inquiry to a delivery decision. Check where it waits, who responds, and what happens when a partner cannot proceed. Watch actual projects and participant outcomes before judging the channel's value.

> [!TIP] Follow one proposal
>
> Test a partnership with a real next step, then learn where the handoff stalls.

**Next question:** What happens to a useful proposal when its first partner cannot carry it forward?

## Leaders

A dependable organization gives people a way to continue, return, or seek correction. Leaders can start by asking those closest to the work where the normal route fails. Their answers should shape a small test with an explicit cost and a review date.

Farnam Street's October 18 issue adds a reflection on how ego can bend our view of facts. Use that as a prompt to discuss a failed test without defending a favorite plan. It is a thinking tool, not evidence that these four practices cause better outcomes.

> [!LEADERS] Make repair part of the service
>
> Invite people to show where they got stuck, then give the next test time and support.

## The Tension

Backup capacity takes time and money away from visible new work. An elaborate recovery plan can also become too costly to maintain. Start with the service whose interruption would hurt people most. Test a modest alternative, then decide whether the evidence supports a larger investment.

## Questions Worth Asking

- Which interruption would stop the result people depend on most?
- Who experiences the failure before a dashboard shows it?
- Can someone return or seek correction without starting from zero?
- What did the last rehearsal change in actual practice?
