A team beats its target, but another team inherits the rework. A product wins more customers, but support cannot keep pace with their problems. A public service saves money, but people spend more time finding help. Progress can look different depending on who carries the cost.

Before expanding a plan, name the people who bear its burdens. Then choose one cost you can observe and one change you can test. This week's sources offer four starting points: team rewards, product growth, public services, and business education. Each supports a question to investigate, with limits that matter.

> [!NOTE] Signal: Check Who Carries the Cost of Progress
>
> A better result should survive a close look at who benefits and who pays.

## What To Do Next

Review one promising plan with the people it affects:

1. **Name the gain.** State the result you want and who receives it.
2. **Find the burden.** Ask who spends extra time, absorbs risk, or loses access.
3. **Compare a change.** Choose one way to reduce that burden while preserving the useful result.
4. **Set a review.** Decide what evidence would justify keeping, changing, or stopping the plan.

Start with an observed cost. Do not assume every complaint has the same cause or that every tradeoff can disappear.

## What This Looks Like

A manager might review a sales reward alongside the extra work sent to support. A founder might read complaints from first-time customers before entering another market. An educator might ask students to revisit a profitable choice after hearing from the people affected.

These are proposed local tests. Their value depends on what you learn and whether the next decision changes.

## 1. Check which rewards make colleagues bear the cost

Stanford's October 22 interview with Nir Halevy reports ideas from his book on hierarchy. The book draws on more than 100 interviews and social-science research. He describes intense competition, unfairness, and instability as recurring problems. His proposed responses include shared value, several ways to contribute, and respectful relationships.

The interview shows why a team problem may require a look at its rules. It does not prove that changing rewards will improve every team's results. Advice drawn from research and interviews still needs a test in the setting where you work.

Choose one reward rule. Ask whether meeting it depends on leaving work, risk, or delay with someone else. Invite that person to describe the cost. Test a change that recognizes useful shared work, and check whether the burden falls or simply moves.

> [!TIP]
> Ask the person receiving the handoff what success costs them.

**Next question:** Which reward looks fair until you include the work another person must do?

## 2. Match growth with the capacity to catch defects

An October 30 Harvard Business Review article reports research on millions of vehicle sales and recalls over a decade. Its visible summary describes how popular products can expose rare defects as more people use them. Wider use brings more situations in which something can go wrong.

The account shows why sales growth deserves attention from the people responsible for quality and response. It does not prove the same pattern holds in every product category. The article summary also leaves limits on what we can assess about the underlying methods.

Before the next expansion, review complaints from newly reached users. Separate minor friction from serious harm. Name who can investigate each warning and how quickly they can respond. Decide whether response capacity can support the next step in growth.

> [!WARNING]
> A rising sales total does not tell you whether customers can get a problem resolved.

**Next question:** Which new user or use condition could reveal a weakness your current measures miss?

## 3. Prepare for the costs that cross service boundaries

UNDP's October 22 report release presents a wider view of security in Asia and the Pacific. It connects trusted institutions, digital safeguards, public health, and disaster preparedness. The report proposes investing in these protections before shocks spread into wider instability.

The release shows where the institution thinks prevention should focus. It does not prove that every proposed measure works in every place. It is a policy analysis and recommendation, not a test of your local service.

Choose a service people depend on. Trace one plausible disruption from the first failure to the next organization. Ask who would face extra travel, delay, or loss of help. Agree on one step that could reduce that burden, then test the handoff with the responsible partners.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> A service boundary should not make the affected person responsible for finding the next owner.

**Next question:** Who carries the cost when responsibility passes from one service to another?

## 4. Teach who pays for a profitable decision

In an October 27 Wharton opinion article, Witold Henisz argues that business schools should account for impact as well as value. He calls for attention to financial, human, social, and natural costs. He also argues that cases can include workers, communities, and regulators alongside the firm.

The article shows a concrete question educators can add to a case discussion. It does not prove that adding stakeholder perspectives changes later business conduct. This is an attributed faculty argument, and the larger changes it proposes need evidence of results.

Choose one case with a clear financial benefit. Ask students to identify an affected group missing from the original decision. Add evidence about that group's costs. Then ask whether the recommendation should change, and what tradeoff remains if it does.

> [!TIP]
> Ask learners to defend the cost they would accept, not just the benefit they hope to create.

**Next question:** Whose evidence could change the answer to an otherwise profitable choice?

## Leaders

A cost review needs permission to affect the decision. People will stop raising burdens if every concern becomes another task for them. Give someone authority to compare alternatives, and explain which costs you will accept, reduce, or refuse.

> [!LEADERS]
> Make the tradeoff visible enough that the people carrying it can question it.

## The Tension

A shared gain can require unequal effort. Refusing every burden can prevent useful work, while ignoring burdens can hide harm behind a good total. The practical task is to make costs visible, compare alternatives, and give affected people a meaningful voice in the choice.

## Questions Worth Asking

- Which cost disappears when we report only the total gain?
- Who can challenge the tradeoff without risking their standing?
- Could we preserve the benefit with less burden elsewhere?
- What evidence would make us slow down or change direction?

## What To Watch Next

Look for evidence that a burden falls, rather than moving out of sight.
