> [!quote] Field Note
> A promise becomes easier to assess when people know what happens next.

> [!summary] The Reader Promise
> Turn one broad promise into a named owner, a visible check, and a date to review the result.

## The Field Note

“We are working on it” gives people little to check. A useful update names the next action and the evidence that would show progress.

SOFX reported that Apple services returned after a widespread outage on November 17. Its report attributes the disruption to an internal update, citing Apple. Recovery raises a practical question for any service team: what check will confirm that the service works for affected users?

The Guardian reported that Cornell announced a review of Greek life, changes to safety programming, and listening sessions. Those are announced actions. They do not establish that future harm will be prevented or that trust has returned. Leaders need to hear from affected people as they assess what changes actually reach them.

Two other sources offer different kinds of next checks. NASA announced a December 7 release of Roman telescope images. That is a dated milestone, not proof of future scientific results. CISA's November 18 vulnerability catalog update gives teams a remediation queue. It does not show whether any particular organization is exposed or compromised.

These cases do not prove a single model of trust. They suggest a useful habit: make the next check specific enough that another person can assess it. Name the owner, the date, and the result that would change the plan. Yesterday's measurement becomes today's follow-up only when someone acts on it.

> [!example] Try this
> Replace “We are improving reliability” with “The service owner will test the affected customer journey tomorrow and report any remaining failures.” Choose a check that fits your actual promise.

## Mental Model Lens

> [!tip] Thinking Tool
> **Mental model:** [The OODA Loop](https://fs.blog/ooda-loop/), an evergreen Farnam Street essay; publication date not stated.
> **Useful idea:** Observe, orient, decide, and act as a repeating loop. Treat each result as information for the next decision.
> **Use today:** Write down the observation that would make you revise your plan. This is a thinking aid, not evidence that a specific response works.

## Use It Today

> [!question] For Students
> Pick one claim in a project update. What could you observe next, and what result would change your conclusion?

> [!question] For Managers And Founders
> Choose one service promise. Name the owner, the customer-facing check, and the time you will report the result.

> [!question] For Educators And Advisors
> Pair one announced policy change with a listening step. Who will hear from affected students, and how will their experience inform the follow-up?

## Reading Room

**Paper:** Oliver Schilke and Martin Reimann (2026), “Trust Beyond Interpersonal Relationships: Unpacking Organizational Trust,” *Annual Review of Organizational Psychology and Organizational Behavior*. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-orgpsych-012825-102948).

Organizational trust involves more than one person trusting another. The authors distinguish trust from people toward organizations, from organizations toward people, and between organizations. Their abstract proposes six types and a decision tree for matching research questions with explanations and measures. This card is based on the abstract; the full review method and its empirical limits were not assessed. It does not establish that a particular intervention restores trust. Use the distinction before choosing a response: name who is trusting whom, what they expect, and which part of the organization must provide evidence. Then choose a check that matches that expectation.
