> [!quote] Field Note
> Invite the objection while the decision can still change.

> [!summary] The Reader Promise
> Turn one meeting update into a decision question, invite the strongest objection, and record what would change your choice.

## The Field Note

> [!example] Try this
> Ask which fact would make the preferred option a poor choice, then give someone responsibility for checking it.

A quiet meeting can feel like agreement. It can also mean that nobody wants to challenge the person who has already spoken.

In an October 7 Harvard Business Review article, organizational psychologist Rebecca Newton describes this problem in executive teams. Her advice includes replacing update meetings with questions that deserve debate. Teams also need trust in one another's intentions, not just confidence in each person's expertise.

This is practice guidance informed by coaching experience and cited research. It does not prove that more disagreement improves every meeting. Conflict over an idea can help a decision; personal attacks can damage the people who must carry it out.

For your next meeting, send routine updates in advance. Put one unresolved choice on the agenda. Ask each person to bring a reason the preferred option might fail. Let people write their views before hearing the most senior person's answer.

Then test the strongest objection. Is it a missing fact, a different goal, or a cost someone else will bear? Record who will resolve it and when the group must choose.

Do not reward objections merely for being clever. Reward the information they add. Watch whether a raised concern changes the plan or leads to a useful check. That is a better sign of honest debate than the volume of discussion.

## Mental Model Lens

> [!tip] Thinking Tool
> **Mental model:** [Think Alone (November 23, 2025)](https://fs.blog/brain-food/november-23-2025/)  
> **Useful idea:** Give an idea a serious challenge before you defend it.  
> **Short quote:** “Attack your best ideas.”  
> **Use today:** Write your strongest objection before the group discussion begins.

This is a practitioner thinking prompt, not evidence that the practice works in every setting.

## Use It Today

> [!question] For Students
> Write one concern before a group project meeting. Explain what evidence would resolve it.

> [!question] For Managers And Founders
> Replace one status update with an unresolved choice. Invite quieter colleagues to contribute before the leader gives a view.

> [!question] For Educators And Advisors
> Ask students to state the strongest case against their preferred answer, then identify the fact that would settle it.
