> [!quote] Field Note
> Growth is safer when the capacity behind the promise is visible.

> [!summary] The Reader Promise
> Name the capacity that must survive before you expand a product, program, or partnership.

## The Field Note

Ferrari presents its first electric car as another Ferrari, which protects a recognizable identity while the product line changes. The World Bank's jobs framework makes a similar point about systems: employment depends on firms, skills, connectivity, regulation, and policy sequence working together.

AACSB's survey of participating deans shows that institutional leaders face pressure from learners, employers, finance, fundraising, and public scrutiny. Kauffman's current workforce stories also describe capacity building as part of a durable pathway. These sources describe strategies and pressures. They do not prove that any one expansion will succeed.

Before you approve growth, name the promise that must stay stable and the capability that must be added. Assign an owner to that capability and choose the measure that will show whether it works.

> [!example] Try this
> Write three lines before approving growth: the promise to protect, the capacity to build, and the evidence that would show the new capacity is working.

> **Use today:** Compare a promising expansion story with the full cohort, customer, school, or firm population before calling it scalable.

## Use It Today

> [!question] For Students
> Ask what evidence supports a pathway's typical outcome, not only its strongest story.

> [!question] For Managers And Founders
> Separate a new feature from a changed customer promise, then assign an owner to the bottleneck.

> [!question] For Educators And Advisors
> Measure pathway participation and completion separately from institutional intent.

## Reading Room

**Paper:** Shourkaei and Dyck (2025), *Organization*. [Scaling sustainability in businesses with a post-growth orientation](https://doi.org/10.1177/13505084251350847).

Based on the abstract, this exploratory study examined 35 small and medium-sized businesses with a post-growth orientation. The authors identified four approaches to scaling positive social and ecological impact: enhancement, expansion, bridging, and collaboration. That gives teams a useful question before adding size: what kind of impact are we trying to scale? The abstract does not establish which approach works best or prove effects on profits. Its selected businesses also limit transfer to firms pursuing rapid growth. Try comparing a larger operation with a partnership that serves the same need. Name the capacity each option requires, then choose one outcome to track.
