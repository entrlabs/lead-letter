> Count the work that makes an AI result usable.

> Count the cost of making an automated task usable before comparing it with the work it might replace.

## The Field Note

A tool can be cheap to run and costly to make useful. Data preparation, setup, checking, and unusual cases all belong in the comparison.

Brian Eastwood's November 10 MIT Sloan article describes research on computer-vision automation. Computer vision uses models to interpret images. The account explains why setup and customization costs matter alongside the cost of each task. The researchers' institutional abstract also models how repeated tasks can spread fixed costs.

This is a model of a particular kind of work. It does not establish the return from every AI tool or predict savings for your firm. A useful comparison needs your task volume, required accuracy, and checking costs.

Choose one repeated task. Count usable results, setup effort, and time spent fixing mistakes. Compare that full cost with the existing process. Recheck the calculation if task volume falls or exceptions require more human work.

> [!example] Try this
> Define one task, its required accuracy, expected volume, setup work, and ongoing review cost. Compare the resulting cost per usable output with the existing process before expanding the pilot.

## Use It Today

> [!question] For Students
> For a repetitive assignment task, count checking and correction time as well as the time saved generating an answer.

> [!question] For Managers And Founders
> Ask the pilot owner to report usable outputs, exception-handling time, and setup effort alongside the headline time saving.

> [!question] For Educators And Advisors
> Have learners compare a one-off task with a repeated task using clearly labeled hypothetical setup and operating costs.

## Reading Room

**Paper:** Samuel Godadaw Ayinaddis (2025), Artificial intelligence adoption dynamics and knowledge in SMEs and large firms: A systematic review and bibliometric analysis, Journal of Innovation & Knowledge, 10(3), 100682. [Read the paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jik.2025.100682).

Ayinaddis reviews 78 peer-reviewed articles on AI adoption in smaller and larger firms. The abstract organizes adoption conditions into ten dimensions, including customization, data, skills, financial readiness, management support, and partnerships. This account is based on the abstract. This review does not give a reliable payback estimate for a particular firm, and its findings span different technologies and settings. Use the dimensions as a prompt to identify costs your pilot may leave out. Assign an owner to data preparation, model customization, staff learning, and exception handling before deciding that a tool’s subscription price represents the cost of adoption.
