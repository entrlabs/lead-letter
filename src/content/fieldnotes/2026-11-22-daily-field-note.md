> [!quote] Field Note
> If partners can attend the meeting, what can they decide once they get there?

> [!summary] The Reader Promise
> Pick a decision that participants can change, then tell them when and how to exercise that choice.

## The Field Note

Imagine asking students to help design an advising program. They choose evening hours because many of them work during the day. Then the team learns that the staffing budget was set a month ago. The invitation was real. So was the constraint. The students deserved to know about it before giving up an evening.

In a November 16 essay, the Kauffman Foundation asks who defines a problem, controls resources and judges progress. Those questions give a program director a way to test an invitation. Write down one decision you can share and one that is already settled. Tell participants who can change the budget if their proposal needs it. The essay offers a way to examine authority. It does not measure the effects of sharing it.

Access to information raises a similar question. Pew Research Center identified more than 109,000 Flock license plate reader locations on a crowdsourced map. Pew cautions that the inventory is incomplete. The map can help a resident ask an agency about a camera. It cannot establish who sets that agency’s rules for using the data. That takes a separate question and a named decision maker.

> [!example] Try this
> Change one line of your invitation. “You can choose the pilot hours at Thursday’s meeting. The director will be there to answer staffing questions.”

## Use It Today

> [!question] For Students
> Before joining an advisory group, ask which part of its last proposal made it into the final decision.

> [!question] For Managers And Founders
> Give your pilot team one spending decision it can make. What limit should members know before they begin?

> [!question] For Educators And Advisors
> Which course or advising decision could students change this term? Put the date for that decision on the invitation.

## Reading Room

**Paper:** Iwan Hermawan and Gita Hindrawati (2026), “Trust-Based Active Participation in the Relationship of the Role of Information Technology towards Organizational Performance.” [DOI](https://doi.org/10.12688/f1000research.176099.1).

Based on the abstract, the researchers studied 177 millennial owners of technology businesses in Indonesia. They examined how participation, trust and the way a business shares knowledge relate to its use of technology and its performance. The selected sample limits how far a reader can extend the results. The abstract does not establish that introducing a tool improves performance. Try asking the people who use a new system who can correct a bad entry and who gets told about the correction. Those answers tell you something about how knowledge travels in your own workplace.
