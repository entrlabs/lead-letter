> [!quote] Field Note
> The person installing a change and the person relying on it have different things to check.

> [!summary] The Reader Promise
> Choose someone downstream of a change, give them one task to try, and agree on what would pause the rollout.

## The Field Note

A router at one Arkansas radio site might sound like a local concern. In its November 18 account, SOFX reported that 17 of 22 FAA en route centers experienced communications problems. The cause was still under investigation. The report cannot tell us whether a particular test would have prevented the disruption. It does raise a question for anyone changing a shared service. Who can tell whether it still works at the other end?

Take a college reopening enrollment to former students. Higher Ed Dive reported that 37.8 million working-age adults in the United States had left college before earning a credential. That count tells an advisor little about what stands between one student and a return. A college can send an invitation and still leave a returning student unable to find out which credits count.

Before the next invitation goes out, ask an advisor who did not design the process to try it. Give them a realistic transfer-credit question. Can they reach a person who can answer it? If the question keeps circling back to the first office, pause the mailing and fix that handoff.

> [!example] Try this
> At your next rollout meeting, give a colleague the same instructions a first-time user gets. Watch where they have to ask for help.

> Follow a returning student's credit question across two offices. Who can give the student a final answer?

## Reading Room

**Paper:** P.G. Nishant (2026), “Organizational Trust as a Mediator Between Organizational Justice and Organizational Commitment.” [DOI](https://doi.org/10.66715/jirf/2026.v1.i2.7483).

Based on the abstract, the study found that people who reported more fairness at work also reported greater trust and commitment. The study measured these relationships at one point in time. It cannot show which came first, and the abstract gives no estimate of how large the relationships were. Use the finding to frame a question after a difficult rollout. Ask the people who bore the cost whether they knew how the decision was made and who could hear an objection. Their answers can guide a review of that decision.
