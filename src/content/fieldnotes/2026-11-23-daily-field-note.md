> [!quote] Field Note
> When you open last quarter’s dashboard, can you still see the numbers you had when you signed the plan?

> [!summary] The Reader Promise
> Keep a dated copy of the figures behind a decision so you can judge it fairly when estimates change.

## The Field Note

Suppose a service manager used an industry sales report to set staffing for the winter. In March, her team opens the same link to review the plan. The page still carries the government seal. The figure for the same quarter has changed.

That can happen even when everyone copied the figures correctly. With its November advance release, the U.S. Census Bureau revised earlier quarterly services estimates. It incorporated corrections and results from two annual surveys. For the seasonally adjusted total, it also updated the factors used to account for the time of year.

A link tells the next manager where to find the series. It cannot tell her which numbers her predecessor saw. Keep the file beside the decision, with its release date and the row you used. When a revision arrives, update the plan if it warrants a change. You can then review two questions separately. Was the call reasonable at the time? What would you do with the figures available now?

> [!example] Try this
> At your next review, open the original estimate before you open the dashboard. Mark the first cell where the two disagree.

## Reading Room

**Paper:** Dean Croushore and Tom Stark (2001), “Forecasting with a Real-Time Data Set for Macroeconomists.” [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.282592).

Based on the abstract, the authors tested how forecasts looked when they used figures available at the time and when they used later versions. In some tests, the later figures made forecast errors look smaller. The choice mattered even for familiar measures such as the average size of an error. The study concerns economic forecasts. It does not tell you how much a revision will change your sales plan. Try scoring last quarter’s forecast twice, once against the first release and once against the revised figure. Keep both scores and label the dates.
