> A system is not ready because it exists. It is ready when people can access it, act within it, and own its consequences.

> [!summary] The Reader Promise
> Use three questions to test whether a new system is genuinely usable, governable, and owned by a human decision-maker.

## The Field Note

> [!example] Check Access, Authority, and Accountability
> When a new system promises better outcomes, check who can enter it, who can change it, and who remains responsible when it fails.

The World Bank Group describes a guarantee program designed to help local financial institutions expand digital payments. Its projected reach is not an observed result, but the mechanism is useful: sharing a specific risk may let more institutions participate and give more small businesses ways to receive payment.

The same questions apply to institutions. Inside Higher Ed reports that Ohio's Senate Bill 1 moved decisions once handled mainly by university boards, administrators, and faculty toward state-level requirements. That is one state's example, not proof of a universal trend. It does show why formal authority and practical influence should be mapped separately.

AI-enabled teams add a third question. AACSB describes a workflow in which AI assists analysis while people interpret context, make the decision, and remain accountable for the outcome. This is a practice argument, not a controlled study of every team. Its discipline is still useful: do not confuse access to a tool with authority to decide or responsibility for the result.

Use the three questions today: Who can access the system? Who can change the decision or process? Who is responsible for the consequences? The answers reveal whether the promise is supported by an operating path.

These sources do not prove that projected payment gains will occur, that Ohio's model will generalize, or that every AI-enabled team will improve. They support a bounded operating check, not a universal prediction.

## Use It Today

> [!question] For Students
> In a case or group project, name who can access the decision, who can change it, and who owns the result.

> [!question] For Managers And Founders
> Map one customer or team workflow. Mark the access barrier, the decision authority, and the person accountable for the outcome.

> [!question] For Educators And Advisors
> Ask learners to compare formal authority with practical influence before proposing a solution to an institutional problem.
