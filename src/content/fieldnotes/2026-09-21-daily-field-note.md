> [!NOTE]
> Before acting on positive feedback, ask who took part and whose experience you have not heard.

> [!quote] Field Note
> The people easiest to hear from may leave important parts of the story untold.

> [!summary] The Reader Promise
> Learn to spot missing voices in surveys, visits, and team feedback before using them to make a decision.

## The Field Note

> [!example] Practical frame
> Name who is represented, identify one missing group, and ask what you need to learn from them.

A college tour gives you a glimpse of life on campus. The guide is welcoming, the spaces look inviting, and you leave with a strong impression. Before choosing where to study, you still need to know what an ordinary week feels like.

The same question matters when a team reviews glowing feedback. Who answered? Who left before the survey arrived? Who never joined at all? The answers can change how much confidence you place in the result.

On September 21, Inside Higher Ed reported how colleges are investing in visits. Examples include travel support, improved visitor spaces, and personal attention. These choices can help students explore a campus. The reporting does not prove how much a tour itself changes enrollment decisions. Students who visit may already differ from those who do not.

Pew Research Center's September 16 report explains a related challenge in polling. People who answer a survey may not reflect the population the poll aims to describe. Weighting adjusts how much different responses count. Pew reports that political weighting improved accuracy in particular election surveys. It also explains why the choice of comparison matters.

This is not a reason to dismiss a good tour or a useful survey. It is a reason to ask how the picture was assembled. A strong impression and a careful estimate can both leave something out.

Start with a decision you face this week. Write down whose experience you already have. Then name one relevant group you have not heard from. That might be students who declined a visit, customers who left, or colleagues who rarely speak in meetings.

Choose a suitable way to invite their views. A conversation may reveal a question worth testing. It cannot make a small, self-selected group representative of everyone. If a survey will guide a major decision, get help with its design and analysis.

Ask while there is still room to change the plan. Wharton's September 15 persuasion guidance recommends seeking input from affected people before settling on an option. That is practical advice, not proof that consultation always succeeds. Its useful test is simple: what could someone tell you that would actually change your choice?

## Mental Model Lens

> [!tip] Thinking Tool
> **Mental model:** [Survivorship Bias](https://fs.blog/survivorship-bias/)  
> **Useful idea:** Visible successes can hide the experiences of people or attempts that did not make it through.  
> **Use today:** Read feedback from people who stayed alongside what you can learn from people who left.

Farnam Street's essay supplies a thinking prompt. Survey nonresponse and survivorship bias are different problems. In each case, asking how examples were selected helps clarify the limits of what you can conclude.

## Use It Today

> [!question] For Students
> After a campus visit, ask a current student what a normal week is like. Which unanswered question could change your choice?

> [!question] For Managers And Founders
> Choose one decision based on customer or team feedback. Who is missing, and how could you invite their views without assuming they speak for everyone?

> [!question] For Educators And Advisors
> Before changing a course based on survey responses, check who responded. What remains unknown about students who did not?
