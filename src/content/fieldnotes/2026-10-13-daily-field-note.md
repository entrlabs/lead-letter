## The Field Note

> [!example] Practical frame
> Choose one service people depend on. Name its essential work, who can make decisions during disruption, and the support needed to keep it available.

An HBR article asks leaders to define a minimum operating core after a cyberattack. Its practical question reaches beyond technology: which decisions and processes must continue when normal systems cannot be trusted?

Three other sources help extend that question. The World Bank's new Philanthropy Hub aims to connect capital, knowledge, and partners. Kauffman's Red Tail Academy profile describes support for fundraising, curriculum, and steadier revenue. AACSB's research-impact framework asks schools to examine how research contributes to practice, policy, education, and society.

Together, these examples suggest a useful exercise. List the work that must survive disruption. Then identify the partners, capacity, and evidence of use that keep the work valuable.

The sources do not prove a universal method. HBR offers survey-based advice; the World Bank announces a new institution. Kauffman describes a funded program, and AACSB proposes a framework. None guarantees that a particular expansion will succeed.

## Use It Today

> [!question] For Students
> Choose a service you use. Explain which work must continue if its normal tools become unavailable, and who could authorize a fallback.

> [!question] For Managers And Founders
> Write a short disruption plan. Name the essential decisions, their owners, the dependencies, and a realistic test of the fallback.

> [!question] For Educators And Advisors
> Define the intended use of one research or learning project. Identify the partners and support needed, then specify evidence that someone used the work.
