> [!quote] Field Note
> A useful answer does not always require another personal detail.

> [!summary] The Reader Promise
> Choose one data-based decision and test whether fewer personal details can still support a useful answer.

## The Field Note

> [!example] Try this
> Name the decision, remove one unnecessary personal detail, and compare the usefulness of the answer.

Before adding another question to a customer form, name the decision that answer will change. If nobody can name it, the extra detail may create risk without helping anyone.

MIT Sloan's October 19 research summary challenges the assumption that more personal data always creates more value. It reports studies of privacy rules, online media, app developers, and research data. In one study, researchers added privacy noise to data used in 93 published social-science studies. They reproduced about 91 percent of the original results.

Privacy noise means adding controlled uncertainty to make individual records harder to identify. The result suggests that useful analysis can survive some protection. It does not prove that every dataset or decision will remain accurate. The summary also describes short-term costs in some settings.

Try a narrow comparison. Choose one routine question, such as which support topic needs a clearer help page. Compare the answer from the full permitted dataset with an answer using fewer personal details. Set an acceptable error limit before you compare them.

Keep the result that meets the task with less exposure. If the answer changes in a way that could harm someone, stop and examine the difference. An exercise like this needs suitable privacy review; it is not a reason to copy sensitive records into a new tool.

The useful next step is a small test with a clear purpose. Watch whether the simpler view still helps the team make the right decision.

This is a practitioner thinking prompt, not evidence that the practice works in every setting.

## Use It Today

> [!question] For Students
> Check a project survey. For each personal question, write the decision its answer supports.

> [!question] For Managers And Founders
> Choose a routine report and test whether a less detailed version meets its purpose. Keep an explicit error limit.

> [!question] For Educators And Advisors
> Teach students to distinguish collecting information from needing it. Compare what a smaller dataset can and cannot show.
